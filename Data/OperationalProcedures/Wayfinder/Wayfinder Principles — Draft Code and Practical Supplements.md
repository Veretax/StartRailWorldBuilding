**WAYFINDER PRINCIPLES — DRAFT CODE**

These principles do not guarantee the correct answer. They describe how a Wayfinder acts responsibly when the correct answer is incomplete, contested, or unknown. They are guides for judgment, not substitutes for it. 

# 1\. Know the Ground Before You Cross It

No method, spell, tool, or doctrine is correct in every circumstance. Understand the people, history, forces, assumptions, risks, and relevant knowledge before acting—including what may be missing from the official record.  
Preparedness is not having every answer. It is knowing enough to recognize what you do not know.

# 2\. Preserve Agency Before Preserving Structure

Systems exist to serve living beings, not consume them. No solution is sound if it requires turning a person into infrastructure, erasing consent, or removing the ability to withdraw. Preserve not only consent, but the capacity to consent: the ability to understand, choose, refuse, and reconsider. If that capacity has been impaired and it is within your power to help, seek first to restore it toward health rather than exploit its absence.  
Belonging without erasure.

# 3\. Test the Seam, Not Merely the Sides

When two coherent systems conflict, examine the interface between them. Friction is not always failure: some friction creates traction, orientation, feedback, and useful resistance. Too little can leave a system unable to grip or respond; too much can generate heat, wear, distortion, and cascading risk. Learn what the friction is doing before you remove it.  
Facts first. Seams second. Conclusions last.

# 4\. Leave the System Better, Healthier, More Scalable, and Repairable

A Wayfinder does not merely suppress symptoms. Seek solutions that leave the system better, healthier, more scalable, and easier to repair than you found it. Restore healthy cycles, reduce accumulated harm, reuse what remains valuable, and avoid fixes that make future maintenance harder.  
Repair without creating the next catastrophe.

# 5\. Come Ready to Contribute

Wayfinding is reciprocal. Do not enter a crossing assuming others exist only to provide knowledge, access, labor, or solutions. Come prepared to contribute to the relationship as well. What you offer need not equal what you receive or even take the same form: it may be information, labor, expertise, patient attention, an honest willingness to listen, or Wayfinding applied to a problem that matters to them. Reciprocity is not a bribe or a ledger; it is a willingness to invest yourself where you ask others to invest themselves.  
Come willing to give, not only to receive.

6\. Build for Failure Before Success  
Every bridge needs an exit. Every circuit needs a breaker. Every anchor needs a ground. Identify thresholds, failure modes, safe withdrawal, independent observation, and what happens when your assumptions are wrong. Design failure to degrade safely rather than collapse catastrophically.  
Be prepared for the solution to fail.

7\. Hold Truth Without Claiming Ownership of It  
Records, models, traditions, observations, and personal experience are all partial witnesses. Preserve provenance. Record observations separately from interpretations, and mark inference as inference. Invite correction. Do not confuse inherited knowledge with discovery, consensus with truth, or authority with infallibility.  
A Wayfinder is a keeper of the map, never its sovereign.

8\. Steward the Whole Life Cycle  
Every system passes through design, implementation, operation, maintenance, adaptation, and eventually retirement or replacement. Understand which stage a system is in before deciding what it needs. Where succession or transition can reasonably be foreseen, plan for it and preserve the knowledge, relationships, and useful structure worth carrying forward. Where the next stage cannot yet be known, preserve enough flexibility, records, and handoff capacity that those who come after are not forced to begin blind.  
Preserve purpose, not permanence.

9\. Find the People Who Matter  
Every system affects people differently, and the people most affected are not always those with the most authority, visibility, or documentation. Seek those who live with the consequences, carry overlooked knowledge, or have been excluded from the official account.  
Do not confuse importance with rank.

10\. Protect Future Choice  
A Wayfinder should prefer actions that preserve options for the people and systems that come after. Avoid irreversible changes when a reversible one can accomplish the same purpose. Do not solve today’s problem by unnecessarily closing tomorrow’s paths.  
Do not spend the future to purchase the present.  
Short Form  
Know the ground. Preserve the person. Test the seam. Restore the cycle. Come ready to contribute. Prepare for failure. Keep truth faithfully. Steward the life cycle. Find the people who matter. Protect future choice.

11\. Listen to Zathras  
In practice: When someone close to the daily operation of a system raises a concern, stop long enough to understand what they have observed before relying solely on official status reports or senior authority.  
Example: If a custodian, junior technician, student, or local operator reports that a supposedly inactive corridor hums at night, record the observation and investigate the pattern even if the official map says nothing is there.  
Failure mode: Dismissing useful operational knowledge because the speaker lacks rank, complete context, formal vocabulary, or confidence—or treating their observation as proof without testing it.

12\. Do Not Let Authority Outrun Understanding.

A successful result proves that something happened. It does not prove that the operator fully understands it, can repeat it safely, can sustain its load, or should own every related responsibility. Classification must not become automatic authorization, workload assignment, or presumed expertise. Review is not ownership. Assistance is not certification. Refusing to sign what cannot be verified is responsible practice, not refusal to help. Build fundamentals before optimizing around exceptional output.

PRACTICAL SUPPLEMENTS AND EXAMPLES

Purpose  
This section turns the principles into teachable habits. Each principle should eventually include a short practical note, a simple Academy-level example, and a common failure mode.

1\. Know the Ground Before You Cross It  
In practice: Learn what you are interacting with before choosing a method, and identify what important knowledge may be absent from the official record.  
Example: Do not cast on an unfamiliar ward until you know what it does.  
Failure mode: Treating a familiar technique—or an incomplete official record—as automatically sufficient in an unfamiliar context.

2\. Preserve Agency Before Preserving Structure  
In practice: A person must remain able to understand, consent, refuse, reconsider, and withdraw wherever possible. If that capacity is impaired and you can safely help restore it, restoration takes priority over using the impairment to proceed.  
Example: If someone says stop during a link, you stop.  
Failure mode: Preserving the connection or mission by trapping the person inside it, or treating earlier consent as sufficient after the person’s capacity to consent has degraded.

3\. Test the Seam, Not Merely the Sides  
In practice: When two systems interfere, inspect where they meet before blaming either side. Ask whether the friction is useful, harmful, or signaling a deeper problem before trying to eliminate it.  
Example: If two spells interfere, examine their overlap instead of assuming one caster is at fault.  
Failure mode: Treating all friction as damage, removing useful resistance, or fixing one side while leaving the actual interface problem untouched.

4\. Leave the System Better, Healthier, More Scalable, and Repairable  
In practice: Repair causes, not just visible symptoms, and leave the system easier to understand, maintain, adapt, and repair afterward.  
Example: Do not only patch a cracked ward; find out why it cracked.  
Failure mode: Creating repeated dependence on emergency fixes, brittle dependencies, or a repair only you can maintain.

5\. Come Ready to Contribute  
In practice: Enter cooperation prepared to contribute as well as receive. The contribution may be knowledge, labor, attention, listening, advocacy, or Wayfinding applied to a problem that matters to the people helping you.  
Example: If you ask another faction or discipline for help, listen for what they need as well. Your contribution might be useful information, patient attention, practical assistance, or helping them understand a problem they have been unable to map.  
Failure mode: Treating local experts as resources to be consumed, or turning reciprocity into a transaction where your contribution is merely leverage for access.

6\. Build for Failure Before Success  
In practice: Know how an experiment, bridge, spell, or device can be stopped safely before activating it, and what safe degraded state it should fall into if full shutdown is not possible.  
Example: Before powering a new device, identify its shutdown method and safe withdrawal path.  
Failure mode: Designing only for the expected success case, or allowing partial failure to cascade into catastrophic collapse.

7\. Hold Truth Without Claiming Ownership of It  
In practice: Track where knowledge came from and distinguish observation, inference, memory, and hypothesis. Record what happened separately from what you believe it means.  
Example: If an idea came from another person, say so; if you are guessing, mark it as a guess.  
Failure mode: Mistaking confidence, consensus, authority, or an old interpretation for proof.

8\. Steward the Whole Life Cycle  
In practice: Understand whether a system needs maintenance, adaptation, replacement, or retirement before deciding what responsible care requires. Plan transitions where they can reasonably be foreseen; where they cannot, preserve flexibility, records, and handoff capacity so future stewards can adapt responsibly.  
Example: If an old ward can no longer be repaired safely, plan a controlled replacement instead of patching it forever.  
Failure mode: Preserving a system merely because it has survived, replacing it merely because it is old, or assuming you can predict a future transition precisely enough to lock later stewards into your plan.

9\. Find the People Who Matter  
In practice: Identify the people who actually experience the consequences of a system, especially those whose knowledge or concerns are easy to overlook.  
Example: If a ward keeps failing, ask not only the ward engineer but also the students, custodians, and others who use the space every day.  
Failure mode: Listening only to the people authorized to speak about a system instead of the people actually experiencing it.  
10\. Protect Future Choice  
In practice: Prefer reversible actions when they can accomplish the same purpose, and consider what choices your intervention leaves to those who come after.  
Example: Stabilize a damaged ward temporarily rather than permanently rewriting it when there is still time to study the cause.  
Failure mode: Solving an immediate problem by unnecessarily eliminating safer or better options later.

11\. Listen to Zathras  
A Wayfinder should pay attention to the people who quietly keep systems functioning: maintainers, custodians, operators, archivists, technicians, local experts, and anyone else whose work is easy to overlook. A “Zathras” may not know the whole strategic picture, but may know the one failing pipe, recurring anomaly, buried dependency, or practical constraint that tells you where to look next. Respect that knowledge. Ask what they have seen. Listen before assuming rank, credentials, polish, or visibility determine whose observations matter.

The name comes from Zathras, the overworked caretaker of an enormous ancient machine: responsible for keeping countless systems running, rarely listened to, and often left alone with knowledge others only notice when something breaks. The lesson is not that the quiet maintainer is always right. It is that proximity to the work creates knowledge. A Wayfinder should neither romanticize nor dismiss it; test it, preserve its provenance, and let it guide the next question.

Listen to the person who knows where the system actually hurts.
